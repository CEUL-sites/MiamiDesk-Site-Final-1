Most sellers prepare for the inspection. Fewer prepare for the underwriter. In South Florida, the question that most often reprices a deal late is not what a home inspector found — it is whether the buyer can obtain property insurance on the home at a cost they are willing to carry.

A financed buyer cannot close without binding coverage. That makes the insurance application a second, quieter review of the property, run by a party the seller never meets, on a timeline that lands after the inspection period is spent and after the buyer's leverage has quietly grown. Sellers who understand what that review looks at can resolve most of it before the property is ever public.

## Why Insurance Problems Surface Late

The order of operations works against the seller. Contract first, inspection next, then appraisal, and only then — often after the buyer has already committed money and time — the insurance application, the carrier's questions, and the quote.

By the time a carrier declines the roof or returns a premium the buyer did not budget for, the seller is deep into the contract with a shrinking calendar. The available responses at that point are narrow: a price reduction, a repair credit, a roof replacement under deadline, or a cancellation and a return to market carrying days on market the property did not earn. None of those are as inexpensive as answering the same questions in advance.

> The seller's leverage is highest before the property is public and lowest in the last two weeks before closing. Insurance questions have a habit of arriving in the second window.

## What the Carrier Is Actually Looking At

Underwriting standards differ by carrier and change over time, and a licensed Florida insurance agent is the right person to confirm current requirements for a specific property. That said, the same categories of information come up on nearly every South Florida application.

**Roof age, material, and permit history.** This is the dominant variable in South Florida underwriting. Carriers evaluate the roof's age and remaining useful life, the covering material, and whether the last replacement was permitted and closed out properly. A roof approaching the end of its practical life is the single most common reason a buyer's application stalls.

**The wind mitigation inspection.** Florida uses a standardized uniform mitigation verification inspection, performed by a qualified inspector, that documents roof shape, roof deck attachment, roof-to-wall connections, secondary water resistance, and opening protection such as impact glass or shutters. Homes that document these features may qualify for meaningful premium credits. A missing or expired report does not fail a home — it just means the property is quoted without credits it may actually deserve.

**The four-point inspection.** For older homes, carriers commonly request a review of four systems: roof, electrical, plumbing, and HVAC. Federal Pacific or Zinsco panels, cloth or aluminum branch wiring, polybutylene supply lines, and aging water heaters are recurring flags.

**Flood zone and elevation.** Flood coverage is separate from a standard homeowners policy. The flood zone designation and, where relevant, an elevation certificate affect what the buyer will pay to carry the property — and a current certificate in the seller's file can meaningfully shorten the buyer's diligence.

**Loss history.** Prior claims on the property follow the address, not the owner. A seller who knows the property's claims history can address it in context rather than let the buyer discover it as a surprise.

## The Condo Version of the Same Problem

For condominium sellers, insurability sits mostly at the association level. The master policy, the association's financial condition, deductible structure, and the status of structural inspection and reserve funding obligations all shape what a buyer's lender and carrier will accept on an individual unit.

This is why association documents have become a pricing factor in their own right, not a formality delivered late in the process. ## What a Seller Can Resolve Before Listing

1. **Establish the roof's documented age and permit status.** Locate the permit, the final inspection, and the warranty if one exists. If the last work was never closed out with the municipality, start that process early — it takes longer than sellers expect.
2. **Order a current wind mitigation inspection.** It is inexpensive relative to its effect on the buyer's carrying cost, and it converts an unknown into a document.
3. **Assemble the insurance file.** Current declarations page, claims history, wind mitigation report, survey, and elevation certificate where applicable, in one place, ready to hand over.
4. **Ask your own agent the buyer's question.** A licensed insurance professional can tell you how the property is likely to be underwritten for a new owner — which is a different question from what you currently pay on a policy you may have held for years.
5. **Address the cheap flags.** Aging water heaters, missing GFCI protection, visible plumbing leaks, and deferred electrical items are far less expensive to correct on your schedule than to negotiate under contract.
6. **Decide the position on the roof before an offer forces it.** Replace, credit, price accordingly, or document why the roof is sound. Any of those can be a defensible strategy. Discovering the question at week six is not.

## How This Changes the Negotiation

Buyers do not pay a premium for uncertainty. When insurability is undocumented, a buyer prices the worst plausible version of the answer into their offer — or into their post-inspection request. When the documentation is complete and available at launch, the conversation moves from speculation to evidence, and the seller is answering questions instead of absorbing assumptions.

## FAQ

### Should I replace the roof before selling?

It depends on the roof's condition, its documented age, the price point, and the competing inventory. Replacement, a negotiated credit, and a price adjusted to reflect the roof can all be reasonable strategies. The mistake is not choosing — it is being forced into one of them by a buyer's carrier at week six.

### Does a wind mitigation report lower the premium?

It may. The inspection documents construction features that many carriers credit. It does not change the property; it records what is already there. A licensed Florida insurance agent can explain how a specific report affects a specific quote.

### Is insurance a reason a South Florida deal falls apart?

It is one of the recurring late-stage risks in this market, alongside appraisal and association document review. It is also among the most preventable, because nearly all of the underlying information can be gathered before listing.

### Can you tell me what my home will cost to insure?

No. Insurance questions should go to a licensed Florida insurance professional, and legal, tax, and association questions to the appropriate licensed advisers. What a listing strategy can do is make sure those answers exist before a buyer's timeline depends on them.
