Selling a condo with a special assessment in Florida is legal and common in 2026, but the assessment travels with the unit. Under section 718.116 of the Florida Statutes, a buyer becomes jointly and severally liable with the seller for unpaid assessments that came due up to the transfer of title. In South Florida, the question is not whether the assessment will surface. It is how the seller documents it, prices it and allocates it in the contract.

An assessment is a fact about the building. *How it appears in the sale is a decision the seller still controls.*

## Can You Sell a Florida Condo With a Special Assessment?

Yes. Nothing in the condominium statute prevents an owner from selling a unit while a special assessment is pending, payable in installments or partly unpaid. What the statute does is make sure the assessment cannot disappear at closing.

The pressure behind these sales is real. On November 10, 2026, [CBS Miami reported](https://www.cbsnews.com/miami/news/hollywood-carriage-hills-condo-special-assessments-foreclosure-united-way-farthing/) that mounting condo association assessments are hitting South Florida seniors particularly hard, describing an owner in a 50-year-old building "undergoing extensive, necessary renovations." Many owners in that position are now weighing whether to pay, borrow or sell.

Much of the current wave traces to the reserve rules in [section 718.112](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/718.112). A residential association must have a structural integrity reserve study, known as a SIRS, completed at least every 10 years for each building that is three habitable stories or higher. The study covers the roof, the structure and load-bearing walls, fireproofing and fire protection, plumbing, electrical systems, waterproofing and exterior painting, and windows and exterior doors. Associations that existed on or before July 1, 2022 were required to complete one by December 31, 2025. For budgets adopted on or after December 31, 2024, the owners of an association that must obtain a study may not vote to provide no reserves, or less than the statute requires, for those items.

The statute also says how the money can be raised: reserves for those items "may be funded by regular assessments, special assessments, lines of credit, or loans," and a special assessment, line of credit or loan for that purpose requires a majority vote of the total voting interests of the association. For many older South Florida buildings, that is the mechanism behind the bill an owner is now holding.

## Who Pays the Special Assessment When a Florida Condo Sells?

Two separate questions are often confused here: who the association can collect from, and how the seller and buyer divide the cost between themselves.

The first is settled by statute. Section 718.116 makes each owner liable for all assessments that come due while he or she owns the unit, and makes a new owner "jointly and severally liable with the previous owner for all unpaid assessments" that came due up to the time of transfer of title. The association also holds a lien on each condominium parcel to secure payment of assessments. That shared liability is why the estoppel certificate, described below, sits at the center of every condo closing file.

The second is a matter of contract. The statute decides whom the association may pursue. It does not decide which party to the sale bears an assessment that has been approved but is not yet due. As a Florida attorney told [CBS Miami in 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/miami/news/im-not-sleeping-at-all-more-hollywood-condo-owners-said-to-be-blindsided-by-steep-fees/), whether a seller must pay depends on the contract language and on what was known or disclosed. The common structures look like this:

- **Seller pays in full at closing.** The remaining balance is paid from the seller's proceeds. Tends to fit when the seller wants a clean file and a buyer pool that includes cautious and financed buyers.
- **Buyer assumes remaining installments.** The buyer takes over future installments, usually against a price adjustment. Tends to fit when the assessment is approved, defined in amount and payable over time.
- **Credit at closing.** The seller credits the buyer for part or all of the remaining balance. Tends to fit when the parties agree on price but not on who carries the cost.
- **Price reflects the assessment.** The list price is set below comparable units without one. Tends to fit when the building's assessment is widely known in the market.

None of these is automatically correct. The right structure depends on the amount, the payment schedule, the buyer's financing and how the property compares with units in the same building that carry the same obligation.

## What the Buyer Will See: Estoppel Certificate and Resale Documents

Florida law puts the assessment in front of the buyer through two channels. A seller who reads both before listing will not be surprised by either.

**The estoppel certificate.** Under section 718.116, the association must issue an estoppel certificate within 10 business days after receiving a written or electronic request from the unit owner or the owner's designee. Among other things, the certificate must provide an itemized list of all assessments, special assessments and other moneys owed on the date of issuance, and an itemized list of any additional assessments and special assessments scheduled to become due during its effective period. A certificate that is hand delivered or sent electronically has a 30-day effective period; one sent by regular mail has a 35-day effective period. The closing figures come from this document.

**The resale disclosure package.** Under [section 718.503](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/718.503), as published in the 2025 Florida Statutes, a buyer purchasing from an owner who is not the developer is entitled, at the seller's expense, to current copies of:

- the declaration of condominium, the articles of incorporation, and the bylaws and rules of the association;
- the annual financial statement and annual budget;
- the inspector-prepared summary of the milestone inspection report, where applicable;
- the association's most recent structural integrity reserve study, or a statement that the association has not completed one;
- the turnover inspection report, where applicable;
- the "Frequently Asked Questions and Answers" document, plus a governance form prepared by the state.

The contract must carry the statute's prescribed language. If the buyer has not received these documents in time, the contract is voidable by the buyer by written notice during a 7-day period, excluding Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays. A seller who delivers a complete package early removes that uncertainty from the transaction.

## Approved or Proposed: Reading the Assessment Before You Price

Not every assessment is at the same stage, and buyers price the stages differently.

Under section 718.112, written notice of a board meeting at which a nonemergency special assessment will be considered must be mailed, delivered or electronically transmitted to owners and posted on the property at least 14 days before the meeting. The notice must state that assessments will be considered and give the estimated cost and a description of the purposes. Under section 718.116, once an assessment is approved, its specific purpose must be set out in a written notice to every owner, and the funds collected may be used only for that purpose. Any excess after the work is completed is treated as common surplus.

Three situations follow from those rules:

- **Approved and scheduled.** The amount and installments are defined. Buyers can underwrite it, and the estoppel certificate will show it.
- **Noticed but not yet voted.** The estimated cost is on paper, but the final number is not. Buyers tend to assume the higher figure.
- **Discussed but not noticed.** A reserve study, a milestone inspection or board minutes point to future cost. Buyers who read the documents will see it, and some will ask for a price that covers it.

The second and third stages carry the most pricing risk, because uncertainty is harder for a buyer to underwrite than a known cost. *A defined assessment is a line item. An undefined one becomes a discount.*

## How a Special Assessment Changes the Price Conversation

Buyers in South Florida's condo market evaluate the total cost of ownership, not only the purchase price. A unit with a large remaining assessment competes with units in buildings that have already completed their work, and with units in the same building whose owners have already paid.

That comparison is the starting point for pricing. The relevant comparables are recent sales in the same building, before and after the assessment was approved, and current listings that carry the same obligation. A pricing analysis that ignores the assessment invites a buyer to price it for the seller, usually at the high end of the estimate.

There is also a case for the seller. A building that has funded its reserves and completed its structural work leaves a buyer with fewer open questions than one still facing that work. Where the assessment pays for completed or contracted repairs, the documents can show a buyer what the money bought. ## A Seller's Preparation Checklist

1. Request the current assessment notice, the board minutes that approved it and the payment schedule from the association.
2. Request the most recent structural integrity reserve study and, where applicable, the milestone inspection summary, so the disclosure package is complete before the first showing.
3. Ask the association for a preliminary estoppel figure, so the remaining balance and scheduled installments are known before pricing.
4. Decide with your agent which structure you will offer: payment at closing, assumption by the buyer, a credit or a price adjustment.
5. Gather recent sales in the same building from before and after the assessment, to anchor the price to evidence rather than to a buyer's estimate.
6. Ask a Florida real estate attorney to review the contract language on assessments before you sign a listing agreement or accept an offer.

## Where Distribution Fits

Features describe a property. Distribution determines its price. Listing on the Miami MLS places the property inside the daily working ecosystem of 93,000 Miami and South Florida REALTORS® members (membership reported in May 2026), who can find it and present it to their own buyers. For a unit with an assessment, that matters because the buyers who can absorb a known cost, including cash buyers and owners consolidating in the same building, are more likely to be found by agents working the full market. Distribution does not guarantee a price or a timeline. It determines how many qualified agents and buyers are in a position to evaluate the unit with the assessment fully disclosed.
