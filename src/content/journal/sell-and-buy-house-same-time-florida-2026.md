To sell and buy a house at the same time in Florida in 2026, an owner chooses between three structures: sell first and stay on through an extended closing or a post-closing occupancy, buy first with a bridge loan or a HELOC, or make the purchase contingent on the sale. With the 30-year fixed rate at 7.11% and Florida taxing every new mortgage when it is recorded, borrowing the gap costs more than many owners expect.

The question is rarely whether an owner can afford the next home. It is whether the equity in the current one can be released in time to pay for it. *The structure decides who carries the overlap, and what it costs.*

## How to Sell and Buy a House at the Same Time in Florida: The Three Structures

Every simultaneous move in Florida comes down to one of three sequences. Each solves the timing problem by shifting a cost or a risk to a different place.

| Structure | How it works | What it costs | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sell first | Close the sale, then stay on through an extended closing or a written post-closing occupancy, and buy with the proceeds | No new financing on the gap; the usual seller closing costs | A short occupancy window if the next purchase slips |
| Buy first | Borrow against the current home with a bridge loan or a HELOC, buy, then sell | Interest plus Florida's taxes on the new mortgage | Two housing payments until the sale closes |
| Sale-contingent purchase | Offer on the next home subject to selling the current one | No new financing | Many sellers decline the contingency, or price it in |

Most South Florida owners who are downsizing, or moving within the region, end up choosing between the first two. The third works in some segments and not in others, as discussed below.

## What Florida Charges When You Borrow the Gap

Florida taxes a new mortgage when it is recorded, which is a cost national guidance on bridge loans and HELOCs usually leaves out. Two state taxes apply.

- **Documentary stamp tax on the mortgage.** [Section 201.08](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/201.08) sets the tax on mortgages and other evidences of indebtedness recorded in Florida at 35 cents on each $100 or fraction of the debt. The same section caps the tax at $2,450 for the unsecured notes described in paragraph (1)(a); the paragraph governing recorded mortgages carries no such cap.
- **Nonrecurring intangible tax.** [Section 199.133](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/199.133) imposes a one-time tax of 2 mills on each dollar of an obligation secured by a mortgage on Florida real property. Under [section 199.135](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/199.135) it is due when the mortgage is presented for recording, and the lender may pass it on to the borrower.

For a closed-end bridge loan, both taxes fall on the full amount at recording:

| $500,000 closed-end bridge loan | Rate | Tax at recording |
|---|---|---|
| Documentary stamp tax, s. 201.08 | 35 cents per $100 | $1,750 |
| Nonrecurring intangible tax, s. 199.133 | 2 mills per dollar | $1,000 |
| Total state taxes | | $2,750 |

That $2,750 is paid before any origination fee, appraisal, title charge or interest, and it is not refunded when the bridge loan is paid off a few months later with the sale proceeds. The figures are the statutory calculation on an illustrative loan amount; a lender's closing disclosure shows the exact charges for a specific loan.

## Why a HELOC Is Taxed Differently From a Bridge Loan

A home equity line of credit is not taxed the same way as a closed-end loan, and the difference matters to an owner deciding how much to borrow.

For the intangible tax, [section 199.143](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/199.143) provides that when a mortgage secures a line of credit, the tax is paid at recording on the maximum amount of the line, and no further intangible tax is due on borrowing under it. A $500,000 line therefore carries $1,000 of intangible tax at recording, whether or not it is ever drawn.

For documentary stamp tax, section 201.08 treats a mortgage that secures future advances differently: the tax is paid at recording on the initial debt, excluding future advances, and then on each advance as it is made. Under that rule, a $100,000 draw on the line carries $350 of documentary stamp tax when it is advanced.

Two practical consequences follow. Sizing the line generously costs intangible tax on the full line from the start. And whether a particular HELOC is documented as a future-advance mortgage is a question for the lender and the closing agent, not an assumption to build a budget on.

## The Qualification Problem: Two Housing Payments on One Application

Taxes are the smaller issue for many buy-first plans. Qualifying is the larger one.

The [Fannie Mae Selling Guide](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-05/monthly-debt-obligations) states that when a borrower obtains a bridge or swing loan to close on a new principal residence before the current residence is sold, the bridge loan is a contingent liability that must be included in the borrower's debt-to-income ratio. Fannie Mae waives that requirement only when the lender receives a fully executed sales contract for the current residence and confirmation that its financing contingencies have been cleared.

In plain terms, a buyer financing the next home with a conventional loan who has not yet sold may have to qualify while carrying the current home and the bridge debt together. Owners with substantial equity but modest current income, a common profile among long-term South Florida homeowners preparing to downsize, can find that ratio harder to meet than the equity would suggest.

The rate environment adds weight to the decision. The [Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms) reported the 30-year fixed rate at 7.11% as of November 12, 2026, against 6.24% a year earlier, and the 15-year fixed rate at 6.45%. Borrowing to bridge the gap means carrying debt at those levels, even briefly.

## Selling First: Extended Closing or Post-Closing Occupancy

Selling first removes the financing question. The owner closes, receives the proceeds, and buys the next home with cash or a smaller new mortgage. The difficulty is where to live in between, and there are two common answers.

- **An extended closing.** The seller negotiates a later closing date in the purchase contract, so the sale closes closer to the date the next home is ready. Ownership, insurance and risk stay with the seller until closing. The trade-off is that a buyer must agree to wait, and a longer contract period leaves more time for a financing or appraisal issue to surface.
- **A post-closing occupancy.** The sale closes on schedule and the seller stays for a defined period under a separate written agreement with the buyer. Ownership and the buyer's insurance obligations start at closing, so the occupancy terms, the insurance position and any escrowed funds need to be in writing before closing. Our earlier guide to [post-occupancy agreements in South Florida](/journal/post-occupancy-agreement-south-florida-2026) covers what to negotiate, and both arrangements belong with a Florida real estate attorney.

Selling first does not avoid the seller's own closing costs. The [Florida Department of Revenue](https://floridarevenue.com/taxes/taxesfees/Pages/doc_stamp.aspx) sets documentary stamp tax on deeds at 70 cents per $100 of consideration in every county except Miami-Dade, where the rate is 60 cents per $100 plus a 45 cents surtax that does not apply to a single-family dwelling. Those costs are part of every sale; the point is that selling first adds no gap-financing cost on top of them. Our [guide to seller closing costs in South Florida](/journal/seller-closing-costs-south-florida-2026) sets out the full list.

## A Sale-Contingent Purchase: When It Works in South Florida

The third structure asks the seller of the next home to accept an offer that depends on the buyer's own sale. It costs nothing to write, but it transfers the timing risk to that seller, and sellers price it accordingly.

It tends to work where the next home has been on the market for some time, where the seller has no competing offers, or where the buyer's current home is already under contract with its financing contingencies cleared. In tight single-family segments, where competing buyers can close without a contingency, it is often the weakest offer on the table. An owner whose current home is priced correctly and prepared for market is in a stronger position to use it, because the contingency is more likely to be satisfied.

## Which Structure Fits: A Decision Checklist

Before choosing, an owner should be able to answer six questions, ideally with a lender, a closing attorney and a tax adviser involved:

1. **Can I qualify for the next purchase while still owning the current home?** If not, a buy-first plan may not be available on conventional terms until the current home is under contract.
2. **How much equity do I need released, and when?** That figure sets the size of any bridge loan or line, and therefore the state taxes on it.
3. **What does the gap cost in each structure?** Compare interest and recording taxes on a bridge loan or HELOC against the cost of a short occupancy or temporary housing.
4. **How strong is demand for my current home?** In a short-supply segment, a seller can often negotiate an extended closing or a post-closing occupancy as part of the sale.
5. **How flexible is the next purchase?** A resale with a motivated seller may accept a contingency; a competitive listing usually will not.
6. **What are the tax and homestead consequences of the timing?** Those questions belong with a tax adviser and, for homestead matters, with the county property appraiser.
