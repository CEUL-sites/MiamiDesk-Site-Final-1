Florida flood disclosure for home sellers is a written, statutory step. Under section 689.302 of the Florida Statutes, a seller of residential real property must complete a flood disclosure and give it to the buyer at or before the time the sales contract is executed. For South Florida owners selling in 2026, that means questions about flood damage, flood insurance claims and flood assistance are answered in writing before any contract is signed.

The form itself is short. *The record behind each answer is what a prepared seller assembles before the property goes public.*

## What the Florida Flood Disclosure for Home Sellers Requires

The obligation sits in [section 689.302 of the Florida Statutes](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/689.302), titled "Disclosure of flood risks to prospective purchaser." It does two things. It places the duty on the seller, not on the agent or the buyer. And it prescribes the form: the statute sets out the disclosure language itself, so the seller is answering fixed questions rather than drafting a narrative.

The current version reflects an expansion that took effect in October 2025. As [Florida Realtors' legal team summarized it](https://www.floridarealtors.org/news-media/news-articles/2025/06/new-flood-disclosure-requirements-florida), residential sellers must now also disclose whether they are aware of flood damage to the property during their ownership, in addition to the earlier questions about insurance claims and assistance.

Three practical consequences follow for an owner preparing a sale:

- **The answers are in writing and signed.** A verbal assurance during a showing is not a substitute, and the written answer travels with the transaction file.
- **The questions are about the seller's own ownership period.** The form asks what the seller knows and has done, not about the property's entire history.
- **The disclosure is separate from price.** It does not ask the seller to estimate risk, cite a flood zone or forecast insurance costs. Those questions still come from the buyer, the lender and the insurer, but they arrive on their own track.

## What the Form Asks, Line by Line

The statutory form opens with a heading, "FLOOD DISCLOSURE," and a flood insurance statement before any question is asked. That statement tells the buyer that homeowners' insurance policies do not include coverage for damage resulting from floods, and encourages the buyer to discuss separate flood insurance with the buyer's own insurance agent.

The seller then answers, by checking a box, the following:

1. **Knowledge of flood damage.** Whether the seller has or has no knowledge of any flooding that has damaged the property during the seller's ownership.
2. **Insurance claims.** Whether the seller has or has not filed a claim with an insurance provider relating to flood damage on the property, including a claim with the National Flood Insurance Program.
3. **Flood assistance.** Whether the seller has or has not received assistance for flood damage to the property, including assistance from the Federal Emergency Management Agency.

The form then defines "flooding" for the purpose of those answers. It means a general or temporary condition of partial or complete inundation of the property caused by any of three things: the overflow of inland or tidal waters; the unusual and rapid accumulation of runoff or surface waters from an established water source, such as a river, stream or drainage ditch; or sustained periods of standing water resulting from rainfall.

That definition is broader than many owners assume. A house that never sat in a named storm surge can still have had water enter from sustained standing rainfall, and the definition is written to capture that. An owner who answers the first question should read the definition first, and then answer it.

## When the Disclosure Is Due, and Why the Timing Matters

The statute sets the timing: at or before the time the sales contract is executed. In practice, the disclosure belongs in the listing file from the start, not in the closing file.

The reason is leverage. A buyer who reads the disclosure before signing prices it into the offer, and the negotiation starts from known facts. A disclosure that surfaces late lands in a different conversation, one in which the buyer already has a contract price and a reason to reopen it.

*Disclosed early, a flood claim is a fact in the file. Discovered late, it becomes a reason to renegotiate.*

There is a second timing point. The buyer's insurance quote, which often depends on flood zone, elevation and claims history, frequently arrives during the inspection and financing period. A seller who has already organized the documents behind the disclosure can answer the insurer's follow-up questions quickly, which protects the contract timeline the buyer agreed to.

## Preparing the Answers Before Listing

The disclosure takes minutes to sign. *The documentation that supports it is what takes time.* Our team asks sellers to assemble the following before the property is listed:

- **Insurance claim history.** Any flood claim filed during ownership, whether with a private carrier or through the National Flood Insurance Program, with the claim outcome and the repair invoices that followed.
- **Assistance records.** Any assistance received for flood damage, including from the Federal Emergency Management Agency, and what it paid for.
- **Repair and permit records.** Contractor invoices, permits and final inspections for any water-related repair, such as drywall, flooring, mechanical equipment or a seawall.
- **Current flood coverage.** The declarations page of any flood policy now in force. Buyers and their insurers routinely ask what coverage exists and at what cost.
- **Elevation documentation.** An elevation certificate, where one exists for the property.
- **A written note of anything uncertain.** If the owner is unsure whether an event met the statutory definition of flooding, that question goes to a Florida real estate attorney before the form is signed, not after.

None of this changes what the form asks. It changes how confidently the seller can answer it, and how quickly the buyer's questions are closed. ## How the Flood Answer Interacts With Insurance and Price

A flood disclosure answer does not set a price on its own. What it does is shape the questions a buyer's agent and insurer ask next, and those questions do affect how the property is valued in negotiation.

In South Florida, insurability is often decided before price. A buyer who cannot obtain an acceptable quote may not be able to close on the terms offered, regardless of how the house shows. We covered that sequence for roof age, wind mitigation and coverage in [Insurability Before Price](/journal/insurability-roof-wind-mitigation-south-florida-sale-2026). Flood is the same discussion with a different set of documents.

The disclosure also interacts with net proceeds. A seller who expects a buyer to ask for a credit after inspection should model that possibility before agreeing on a list price. Our guide to [what it costs to sell a home in South Florida](/journal/seller-closing-costs-south-florida-2026) sets out the line items that sit between the contract price and the amount the seller actually receives.

Buyers do not penalize a property for having a history. They penalize uncertainty. A seller who answers "has" on a claim question and supplies the claim file, the repair invoices and the permit closeout gives the buyer something to underwrite. A seller who answers the same question and supplies nothing gives the buyer a reason to assume the worst.

## If the Property Is Rented: The Landlord's Parallel Form

Owners who rent out South Florida property before selling it should know that Florida also requires a landlord flood disclosure. Under [section 83.512 of the Florida Statutes](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2026/83.512), a landlord of residential real property must complete and provide a flood disclosure to a prospective tenant at or before the execution of a rental agreement for a term of one year or longer, in a separate document.

The two duties run in parallel and are easy to confuse:

**Seller disclosure (section 689.302)**

- Provided by the seller of residential real property, to the purchaser.
- Due at or before the time the sales contract is executed.
- The form's insurance statement: homeowners' policies do not cover flood damage.

**Landlord disclosure (section 83.512)**

- Provided by the landlord of residential real property, to the prospective tenant, in a separate document.
- Due at or before a rental agreement for a term of one year or longer is executed.
- The form's insurance statement: renters' policies do not cover flood damage.

For an owner selling a tenant-occupied property, the practical point is consistency. The answers given to a tenant and the answers given to a buyer should rest on the same records. A buyer's agent who obtains the lease file will compare them.
