South Florida is not one market. A Weston single-family home, a Brickell condominium, and a Fort Lauderdale waterfront property are examined by different buyers, against different documents, for different reasons. The owner who knows what will be checked, and in what order, prepares the sale on their own schedule instead of the buyer's.

This guide sets out eleven markets side by side. For each one it states what buyers and their agents verify first, where transactions most often lose ground, and what moves price. It replaces a series of shorter single-market notes, so the local detail now sits in one place.

## How to Read This Guide

Each market section follows the same order: the setting and the active buyers, the diligence list, the recurring problem, and the price drivers. Owners should read their own market and one neighboring market, because buyers compare across city lines more often than sellers expect.

An item a buyer discovers costs more than the same item disclosed up front. The diligence lists below are the practical starting point for a pre-listing file.

## Weston: The community, not the city

Weston reads as one market from the outside and behaves as a dozen from the inside. The community line matters more than the city line.

The setting is master-planned communities where most homes sit under an association and were built in a compressed window. The most active buyers are relocation, school-driven, and executive-family buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- The community association budget, rules, and any assessment history
- Roof and impact-window condition on original construction
- Permit history for pools, patio enclosures, and window upgrades

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is an association document set that arrives late and contradicts what the listing implied.

**What moves price.** Value follows the specific community rather than the city, lot position and whether it backs to water or preserve, and how completely the interior has actually been updated. Buyers here compare a handful of near-identical floor plans across several communities at once, so condition, updates, and lot position carry more weight than square footage alone.

## Coral Gables: The lot, the block, and permit history

In Coral Gables the lot and the block frequently carry more value than the structure standing on them.

The setting is an older architectural housing stock where renovations and additions have accumulated over decades. The most active buyers are estate, executive, and international buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- Permit history and closed-out municipal records for past renovations
- Any architectural or historic review obligation attached to the property
- Roof, plumbing, and electrical condition typical of older construction

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is a past renovation that was never permitted surfacing in the buyer's municipal and title search.

**What moves price.** Value follows the block and the lot before the house itself, architectural provenance and the quality of past renovation work, and how the property reads against a rebuild on a comparable lot. A buyer at this price point is usually deciding between a renovated older home and tearing down for new construction. The listing either makes that comparison easy or loses it by default.

## Aventura: The building, the line, and the documents

An Aventura buyer is acquiring a share of a building as much as a set of rooms.

The setting is high-rise buildings where the association governs most of what a buyer is actually acquiring. The most active buyers are international, downsizing, and second-home buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- The association budget, reserves, and assessment history
- The building's structural inspection and reserve funding status
- Leasing rules and any restriction on renting the unit out

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is a reserve or assessment question landing after the inspection period has already run.

**What moves price.** Value follows the line and floor within the building, exposure, view corridor, and light, and parking and storage that convey with the unit. Several units in the same building often compete at the same time. What separates them is line, view, condition, and whether the document package is ready when the buyer asks.

## Doral: Identical models and what separates them

Doral inventory repeats itself. The same models list again and again, which makes small differences decisive.

The setting is newer construction where much of the inventory shares a small set of builder floor plans. The most active buyers are LATAM, corporate relocation, and family buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- Association documents and any community development district assessment that applies
- Builder warranty items that are still open
- Permit records for pools, extensions, and impact upgrades

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is competing directly against an identical model listed the same week at a lower number.

**What moves price.** Value follows the community and the specific builder model, lot position and water frontage where it exists, and upgrades that go beyond the builder standard. When several identical models list at once, the floor plan stops being a differentiator. Presentation, upgrades, and terms are what remain.

## Brickell: The building underwriting, not just the unit

Brickell trades like a securities market: same building, same line, different numbers depending on who is selling and why.

The setting is a dense high-rise market where the buyer is underwriting the building as much as the unit. The most active buyers are financial-district, international, and investor buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- Association financials, reserves, and assessment history
- Structural inspection and reserve funding status
- The building's policy on short-term and seasonal leasing

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is a lender or insurance carrier taking issue with the building rather than the unit.

**What moves price.** Value follows line, floor, and view corridor, finish level relative to the building standard, and parking and whether the unit is currently leased. Investor buyers price the unit off what it can carry. Owner-occupants price it off how it lives. A listing that addresses only one of those is competing for half the market.

## Fort Lauderdale: Clearance, dockage, and seawall condition

On the water, the specification a seller never mentions is often the one that decides whether the right buyer books a showing at all.

The setting is a market where water access is a measurable specification rather than a description. The most active buyers are boating, executive, and Northeast relocation buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- Seawall condition and any repair history
- Dock and lift condition, capacity, and permit status
- Bridge clearance and the actual route to the ocean
- Flood zone and elevation documentation

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is seawall or dock condition discovered at inspection and priced by the buyer instead of the seller.

**What moves price.** Value follows usable water frontage rather than total frontage, whether access is ocean-access or restricted by a fixed bridge, and dockage capacity measured against the buyer's vessel. Two homes on the same canal can serve completely different buyers depending on clearance and dockage. A listing that omits those specifics gets filtered out by the buyers it should be attracting.

## Plantation: Uneven updates and system age

Plantation's housing stock has aged unevenly, and buyers price that unevenness precisely.

The setting is established neighborhoods with mature lots and a housing stock that has been updated unevenly. The most active buyers are Broward family and move-up buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- Permit history for additions, enclosures, and system replacements
- Roof and HVAC age measured against what insurers now expect
- Any open or unrecorded municipal items

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is roof or system age turning into an insurability problem late in the contract.

**What moves price.** Value follows the specific neighborhood rather than the city average, lot size and mature landscaping, and how much of the home has genuinely been updated versus refreshed. Buyers weigh an updated home against one they would have to renovate themselves. The distance between those two positions is where most of the negotiation happens.

## Pompano Beach: Which audience the listing is written for

Pompano Beach asks a seller to choose an audience before choosing a price.

The setting is a mixed coastal market where condominium and single-family inventory compete for overlapping buyers. The most active buyers are coastal, investor, and second-home buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- Association financial and structural status for condominium units
- Roof, opening protection, and coastal exposure for single-family homes
- Any leasing restriction that changes what an investor can do

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is pricing to an investor buyer while presenting to a lifestyle buyer, and convincing neither.

**What moves price.** Value follows distance and actual access to the water, building or home condition relative to the rest of the block, and whether the property can be rented, and on what terms. Investor buyers and lifestyle buyers value the same property on different arithmetic. The listing has to be explicit about which case it is making.

## Kendall: The neighborhood, not the wider area

Kendall is not one market. The difference between neighborhoods a few streets apart is real, and buyers know it.

The setting is a large suburban market where neighborhoods a few blocks apart trade on different terms. The most active buyers are Miami family, multigenerational, and local move-up buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- Permit history for additions, enclosed spaces, and in-law conversions
- Association rules where the home sits inside one
- Roof, HVAC, and plumbing age

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is unpermitted enclosed space counted as living area in the listing and removed by the appraiser.

**What moves price.** Value follows the specific neighborhood rather than the wider area, lot size and genuinely usable outdoor space, and whether additions were permitted and count as living area. Multigenerational buyers place real value on space that other buyers discount. How the floor plan is described changes which of them shows up.

## Sunny Isles Beach: Exposure, line, and the document package

In an oceanfront tower, the line and the exposure do most of the pricing work before anyone considers the interior.

The setting is oceanfront towers where the building, the line, and the exposure carry most of the value. The most active buyers are international, luxury, and second-home buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- Association reserves, assessment history, and structural inspection status
- The building's leasing policy
- How oceanfront exposure affects maintenance and insurance on the unit

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is a cross-border buyer stalling on documents that were never assembled in advance.

**What moves price.** Value follows direct versus partial ocean exposure, line and floor within the tower, and finish level and whether furnishings convey. Cross-border buyers frequently evaluate the building before they evaluate the unit, which makes a complete and readable document package part of the pricing strategy rather than paperwork.

## Boca Raton: Community, club obligation, and condition

Boca Raton contains several distinct markets that share a postal address and very little else.

The setting is a market split between association-governed communities, club communities, and open neighborhoods. The most active buyers are executive, downsizing, and Northeast relocation buyers.

**What gets verified first**

- Community association documents and any mandatory club obligation attached to the property
- Roof and impact-opening condition
- Permit records for renovations and additions

**Where transactions lose ground.** The recurring problem is a club or membership obligation surfacing after the buyer has already formed a price expectation.

**What moves price.** Value follows the community, and any membership obligation that transfers with it, lot position and frontage, and renovation level measured against the community standard. A mandatory club obligation changes the buyer pool outright. A listing that leaves it ambiguous attracts the wrong showings and invites renegotiation late.

## What This Means Before Listing

The pattern across all eleven markets is the same. The questions a buyer will ask are known in advance, and the documents that answer them can be assembled before the property is public. A seller who does that work first negotiates from a prepared position. A seller who does not negotiates after the inspection, on the buyer's findings.

The distribution side follows the preparation. A listing enters the MLS ecosystem that South Florida agents work from daily, and cooperating agents bring the relocation, international, and local buyers described above. Exposure does not guarantee a price or a timeline. It determines how many qualified agents and buyers evaluate the property.

## FAQ

### My city is not listed. Does the same approach apply?

Yes. The method is the same in every South Florida market: identify the likely buyer, assemble the documents that buyer will ask for, and price against the specific neighborhood or building. The strategy review covers markets beyond the eleven described here.

### Should I fix every item on my market's diligence list before listing?

Not necessarily. Some items are better repaired, some are better documented and disclosed, and some are more sensibly priced in. That decision depends on the property and the likely buyer, and it is part of the strategy review.

### Should legal, tax, or association questions be handled separately?

Yes. Legal, tax, financing, insurance, association, and homestead questions belong with the appropriate licensed professionals before any binding decision is made.
